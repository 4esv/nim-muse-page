Cash Pays a Different Price Now

The last penny was struck in Philadelphia in November 2025, and Congress finished the job this fall. Cash totals now round to the nearest nickel. Card totals do not. The coin is ending; the cent is not.

OCTOBER 13, 2026

The plastic tray beside the register was the first rounding system. Need a penny, take a penny. Leave a penny, and someone else needs one. It moved single cents from people who did not want them to people who were one short, at no charge, with no form to file. It worked because the amounts were too small to argue about, and because nobody wanted the coin back in a pocket.

That tray is a relic now. On November 12, 2025, the Mint struck its final circulating penny in Philadelphia. In late September 2026, Congress passed the Common Cents Act, ending production for general circulation and giving stores a federal rule for the gap the coin leaves behind: cash totals round to the nearest nickel. Card totals do not round at all.12

Same shelf. Same goods. Two precisions.

The last strike

The first American cents were struck in 1793, large copper coins close in size to a modern quarter, from an era when a cent could buy something on its own. The Lincoln cent arrived in 1909 and outlasted wartime steel and a zinc core. By fiscal 2024, each new penny cost 3.69 cents to make and distribute, nearly four times its face value. The Mint still produced 3.2 billion of them that year, because the existing stock keeps leaking out of circulation into jars, drawers, fountains, and the space under car seats.1

More than a hundred billion pennies remain legal tender. Almost none of them are working. A currency that must be reminted by the billions because its holders keep it in jars is not being saved. It is being stored, at face value, by people who consider it beneath spending.2

The final coins were marked with an omega, the last letter of the Greek alphabet, and assembled into 232 sets, one for each year of production. Auctioned in December 2025, the sets brought in $16.76 million. The last set, sold with the cancelled dies, went for $800,000.3 The penny ended as it had to, worthless in the tray and priceless in the case.

Rounding is not random

The rounding rule itself is plain. A cash total ending in one or two cents rounds down to zero. Three or four rounds up to five. Six or seven rounds down to five. Eight or nine rounds up to ten. Five and zero stay where they are. Applied to totals chosen by chance, the rule is symmetric, and across a year of shopping the ups and downs should cancel to nearly nothing.2

Canada ran the experiment first. Distribution of its penny stopped on February 4, 2013, and the Bank of Canada expected no measurable effect on inflation, a prediction that held. A quieter result held too. A University of British Columbia study of more than 18,000 grocery prices found that rounding moved about 3.27 million Canadian dollars a year from shoppers to grocers, roughly $157 per store.4

The gap lives in the price tags. Rounding cancels only if final digits are spread evenly, and final digits are not spread evenly, because prices are designed, not drawn. They end in nines. A basket built from prices ending in .99 and .98 does not produce random totals, and the rounding of a designed total is not a coin flip. It is a small, steady tilt, too small for any one shopper to fight and large enough, across millions of baskets, to keep.

Rounding is fair to a random basket. Nobody buys a random basket.

Retailers wanted the federal rule anyway, for practical reasons. Some state and city laws barred rounding outright. Grocers handling food assistance faced equal treatment rules against charging one class of customer a different price from another. A clerk with an empty penny drawer and a total of $9.98 needed cover, not philosophy.2

Two kinds of money

Cash is now the coarse instrument in a fine system. The Federal Reserve payment diary found cash in 14 percent of American payments in 2024, a share that had held steady for four years. Households earning under $25,000 a year paid cash for 24 percent of their payments, more than twice the rate of households earning over $100,000. Adults over 55 used cash for 19 percent of theirs.5 The rounding grid lands where cash lives: on small purchases, made most often by the people with the least slack in them.

Card money keeps the cent. So do the tax code, the bank statement, and the interest charge. So does the price of gasoline, which station signs have quoted in tenths of a cent for generations, a denomination no mint ever struck and no driver ever held. America has priced fuel in unminted money for a century without incident. The cent is joining the tenth of a cent: exact, invisible, and unavailable in physical form.

Britain has run this afterlife before. The last guinea was struck in 1813, and the coin was replaced in the recoinage of 1816. Professional fees, land, horses, and art kept being quoted in guineas until decimalisation in 1971, and racehorses are still bid in guineas at auction.6 A unit with habit behind it does not need a coin. It needs people who keep writing it down.

The cent has a country doing that.

Next in line

Rounding promotes the nickel to the smallest unit of American cash, and the nickel arrives with arithmetic of its own. It cost about 13.78 cents to produce and distribute in fiscal 2024, a loss of more than eight cents on every coin, worse per unit than the penny it replaces. The new law lets the Mint cut that cost with a cheaper zinc core inside the nickel. The admission sits inside the same statute that buries the penny: the problem was never one coin. Inflation turns small metal discs into subsidies one denomination at a time, from the bottom up.7

None of this will feel like an event at the register. Two cents here. Three cents there. In both directions, under the threshold of complaint. Denominations die that way, in ten thousand small settlements nobody records. There was a ceremony in Philadelphia, with officials and an omega stamp, and it changed nothing at any register in the country. The tray will adapt to the new grain. Someone will leave a nickel in it. Someone short five cents will be glad it is there.

The coin is in the jar, and the jar is not going back into circulation. The cent is in the ledger, where it will go on being charged, taxed, and compounded to two decimal places indefinitely. America did not abolish its smallest unit of money this fall. It abolished the only version of it that could be held, and kept the version that can be charged.
