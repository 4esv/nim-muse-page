The Teeth Kept the Accounts

In three Peak District cemeteries, the grave goods record what a family could display. The tooth enamel records what a childhood absorbed. Between the two ledgers, a medieval lead industry gets its dates, and its costs get assigned.

NOVEMBER 2, 2026

At Wigber Low, in the Derbyshire hills, one seventh-century grave held a sword. Another, close by, held a pendant of gold worked around a beaver tooth, thirty-seven millimetres long. The objects were placed there on purpose. Somebody chose them, carried them to the grave, and meant them to be read: this family had wealth, reach, standing.

The same graves held a second record, and nobody chose it. It sits in the teeth. A team led by Christopher Loveluck at the University of Nottingham measured the lead locked in the enamel of people buried at Wigber Low and at two cemeteries in Castleton, Joule Yard and Spital Field. The burials span the years from about 650 to 1500. The paper appeared in Antiquity in October.1 Read beside the grave goods, the enamel disagrees with the display in a specific direction. The richer the burial, the cleaner the childhood.

The record nobody edits

Tooth enamel is laid down in childhood, roughly between the ages of four and twelve, and then it stops. It does not remodel. It does not heal over, average out, or take instruction from the adult the child becomes. Whatever the body absorbed in those years, lead included, stays in the enamel at the concentration it arrived with, for the rest of the life and long past it.

Most historical evidence is edited before it reaches us. Chronicles were written by the literate, for patrons. Charters record what institutions wanted remembered. Grave goods are a family statement, staged at the one moment a family controls completely. Enamel is the opposite kind of source. A child cannot curate it. A parent cannot polish it. A priest cannot redact it. It is testimony taken from witnesses who did not know they were giving any.

That makes it a measuring instrument as well as a relic. Pair the enamel reading with a radiocarbon date and a grave becomes a dated sample of the air, the water, and the household work of its decade. String the samples across three cemeteries and eight centuries and the samples stop being biographies. They become an industrial history, written in the bodies of the workforce.

Dating an industry by its children

The Peak District held one of the largest lead industries in medieval Europe, worked since Roman times. After Rome withdrew in the fifth century, production collapsed, and the written record goes thin enough that historians could not say when smelting resumed in earnest. Furnaces leave slag. Slag is hard to date closely. Children are easier.

The enamel puts the revival in the mid-seventh century. By about 750 to 850 the mining and smelting centre at Castleton was polluted enough that every child in the sample took up high levels of lead. The whole community registers. Levels stayed elevated for centuries and peaked in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, when Peak District production reached its maximum.1

The comparison the team reached for is not a gentle one. By the seventh century, children in these mining communities carried lead at levels comparable to children in industrialised cities a thousand years later, in the mid-eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. At times the medieval levels may have run higher than the Industrial Revolution managed.2 Loveluck, describing the readings to the BBC, said the levels were off the scale.3 The soot-black factory town turns out to have a predecessor in a green valley, seven hundred years before the steam engine, with the exposure carried not by factory hands but by households.

Who paid, itemised

In the seventh-century graves the exposure is uneven, and the unevenness follows rank. Men buried with high-status goods showed very low levels of contamination. People buried with no markers of status registered far higher.3 The pattern fits an industry directed from above and breathed in below: the families who profited from the revival of lead production were not the families whose children absorbed the most of it. Oversight, it turns out, is a form of distance.

How did a child take up lead at all? Some of it was ambient, dust and fumes settling over a smelting town until the home itself was contaminated. Some of it may have been work. Loveluck raised the possibility plainly: children helping to process the ore, smashing it into smaller pieces before smelting.3 Enamel cannot distinguish the dust a child breathed from the dust a child raised. It records the total and keeps the circumstances to itself.

By the Castleton centuries the distinction mattered less, because the total was high for everyone in the sample. Scale did what status had done. A small industry can poison a class. A large one poisons a whole valley, and the overseers' grandchildren grow up inside the plume with everyone else.

The bill came due early

The sharpest entries concern women. Three women with high childhood lead died between about seventeen and twenty-five. Across the sample, most men lived to middle or old age, while women mainly died in young adulthood.2 Anna Bloxam, one of the authors, summarised the home side of it: girls and boys alike were raised in dangerously polluted home environments, and the exposure may have contributed to higher rates of premature death among women of childbearing age.2 Lead stored in the body can return to the bloodstream during pregnancy, which puts the exposure of a ten-year-old on the books of a twenty-year-old.

Hold the finding at its proper strength. Three cemeteries are not a nation. An association between childhood lead and early death is not a proven cause of any single burial, and medieval life offered many competing ways to die young. There is a quieter bias built into the instrument itself: everyone in the sample lived to adulthood, so every reading comes from a survivor. The children the lead may have taken earliest left no adult teeth in these graves to measure. The ledger, complete as it looks, is missing its worst page.

Two ledgers

Isotope analysis says most of these people grew up locally, in the district that poisoned them. The industry was stable, the work inherited, the exposure inherited with it. Generation after generation was born beside the smelters, absorbed the valley's main product before adolescence, and was buried in the same ground, some with gold, some with nothing, all of them carrying the reading.

The grave goods say what a family could afford to declare. The enamel says what the declaration cost and who was charged. Archaeology usually works from the first kind of evidence, the deliberate kind, and spends its ingenuity discounting for the staging. Here the second kind survives by accident of chemistry, a tissue that forms once, early, and never takes corrections.

Somewhere in Derbyshire the pendant still reads as it was meant to. Wealth, reach, standing. Underneath it, in the jaw of the same generation, the other account sits in parts per million, itemised by year of childhood, balanced against lives that ended at twenty. Both records are true. Only one of them was voluntary.

The study pairs tooth-enamel lead concentrations with radiocarbon dates across three cemeteries: Wigber Low (seventh century), Joule Yard in Castleton (seventh to eleventh centuries), and Spital Field (twelfth to fifteenth centuries). It dates the post-Roman revival of production to the mid-seventh century, large-scale production including Castleton by the mid-eighth, and the peak of exposure to the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries.
