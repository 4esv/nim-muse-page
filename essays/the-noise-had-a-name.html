The Noise Had a Name

Euclid was launched to map the dark universe, and its survey treats anything moving in the foreground as noise to be stripped out. In September its cleaning software flagged a streak in the Deep Field North. The streak was a comet no catalog contained, 1.3 billion kilometres away, arcing over the top of the Solar System on an orbit of thousands of years.

OCTOBER 21, 2026

In one Euclid exposure the galaxies hold still and one thing does not. The exposure runs 560 seconds. Galaxies billions of light-years away sit for their portrait. The near thing smears. It crosses the frame while the camera is open and leaves a streak, the mark of an object too close and too fast to belong in the picture.1

Between September 16 and 24, the European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope was working its Deep Field North, imaging each field two to four times. Software written by Mikko Pöntinen, a doctoral student at the University of Helsinki, flagged the streak. By predicting where the thing should move next, he found it again in five image sets, sixteen exposures in all. Around the moving point sat a fuzzy cloud. A coma. Dust and gas were coming off an active body, this far from the Sun.12

Built to look past it

Euclid launched on July 1, 2023, and took up a halo orbit around the Sun-Earth L2 point, a million and a half kilometres behind Earth as seen from the Sun. Its assignment runs at least six years and covers more than a third of the sky. It will measure the shapes and distances of billions of galaxies, out to ten billion light-years. Measured closely enough, those shapes weigh the dark matter bending the galaxies’ light. The distances clock the dark energy stretching their separations. Ninety-five percent of the universe is the quarry. The Solar System is the window it looks through.3

The survey is built to ignore the window. Euclid works by step-and-stare, visiting most patches of sky once and moving on, so nothing near can be revisited on demand. It steers away from the plane of the planets, where dust glare would spoil the deep images. Comets live in that plane. The telescope was routed around their neighbourhood on purpose.2

Moving objects still have to be tracked, for a housekeeping reason. A trail crossing a galaxy image corrupts the measurement, so every mover in the field must be identified before its light can be removed. The agency’s own account calls such trails noise. Pöntinen’s software belongs to the cleaning apparatus. It watches the foreground so the foreground can be deleted.2

Straight up

The streak matched nothing. Checks against the catalogs of known comets and asteroids came back empty. The observations went to the Minor Planet Center. Ground-based telescopes picked the object up, and on October 8 it was confirmed and designated C/2026 S1 (Euclid). First new comet in the telescope’s record. It had been sitting in the cleaning queue.2

It is about 1.3 billion kilometres from Earth, a distance on the scale of the run from the Sun to somewhere between Jupiter and Saturn. It is not travelling in the flat. From the Sun’s perspective the comet is currently straight up, arcing over the top of the Solar System on an orbit that will take thousands of years to complete. On its previous approach there were no telescopes. On its next return, Euclid will be long past its six years. It came over the top, through the deep fields, during the eight days the survey happened to be looking there. That was the one path in, for an object crossing in front of a telescope that avoids the planetary plane. “Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid,” the archive scientist Bruno Altieri said. “It was a happy coincidence.”2

The waiting room

A normal mission runs in a fixed order. Find the target. Build the spacecraft. Go. Pristine comets break the order. A body falling in from the far edge of the Solar System, carrying material unprocessed since the system formed, is usually discovered months before it arrives. A spacecraft takes years. By the time anything could launch, the visitor has rounded the Sun and gone.45

So the European Space Agency reversed the order. Comet Interceptor, due to launch in late 2028 or early 2029, will travel to L2, the same halo Euclid occupies, and park. There it waits, up to four years on the fuel it carries, for a suitable comet to be discovered by somebody else. Then it leaves the waiting room and flies out to meet the target on the way in, splitting into three spacecraft shortly before the encounter to read the coma from three points at once. If a suitable object from another star crosses within reach instead, the mission can take that. If nothing comes at all, a known short-period comet waits as the fallback. Even the patience has a deadline, and a plan for the deadline passing.456

C/2026 S1 is not a candidate. Its orbit does not bring it within the Interceptor’s reach. What it supplied was a rehearsal, run by accident, of the whole chain the parked spacecraft depends on: a survey flags a mover, an alert goes out, ground telescopes confirm, a designation lands. Twenty-two days from first frame to confirmed comet. Altieri again: the discovery “gives us a real test case for how our two mission teams will coordinate in the future.” The coordination is the mission. The spacecraft is the part that waits for the coordination to matter.2

Consider what the catalog now holds. For eight days in September a body that had been falling toward the Sun, unrecorded, for longer than telescopes have existed crossed the sightline of a machine designed to remove it from photographs. The removal software gave it a predicted path instead. The path gave it a name in the records of the Minor Planet Center. It will still be filed there, under C/2026 S1, long after Euclid has gone quiet at L2. The galaxies in those frames will not have changed in any way a measurement could catch. Somewhere above the flat of the planets the streak is still travelling, outbound on the long leg now, still shedding its small cloud, still noise in every picture it crosses. It crosses fewer pictures than it used to. One of them kept it.
