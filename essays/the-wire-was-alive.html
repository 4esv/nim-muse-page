The Wire Was Alive

In seafloor sediment, a filament of cable bacteria takes electrons from sulfide centimetres below the surface and spends them at the top, where the oxygen is. The structure carrying that current is a metal-organic framework, a class of materials chemists credited themselves with inventing. The bacteria originated about 600 million years ago.

OCTOBER 22, 2026

A tube pushed into seafloor sediment comes up layered. At the top, a thin skin of mud touches oxygenated water. Below it, the sediment turns airless and rich in sulfide, the rotten-egg compound that collects where organic matter decays without oxygen. Between those two layers sit a few centimetres of mud. For most organisms the distance is nothing. For a cell that needs both sides of a chemical reaction, it is the whole problem.

Cable bacteria live stretched across the gap. They grow as multicellular filaments, centimetres long, standing in the sediment like buried line. Cells deep in the filament oxidise sulfide and release electrons. Cells near the surface take oxygen or nitrate and accept them. No single cell does both halves. The filament does both halves, and a current runs between its ends to close the circuit.1

Two halves, one filament

Breathing, stripped to its accounting, is electron disposal. An organism takes electrons from food, or from a compound like sulfide, and hands them to something willing to receive them. In human cells the donor and the acceptor meet inside the same microscopic compartment. The cable bacterium has separated them by centimetres of mud.

That separation is about 10,000 times the length of a single bacterium, which in human units resembles eating in one town and exhaling in another, with the meal carried between the two as current. The comparison understates the strangeness, because the towns are the same body, and the road between them is also the body, built for the traffic it carries.2

The wires were known before their material was. Researchers had measured the conductivity of cable bacteria filaments and mapped the fibres running along them. What the fibres were made of stayed open. This month a team led by Filip Meysman at the University of Antwerp reported the answer for Candidatus Electrothrix gigas, and the answer belonged to a different field entirely.2

Rooms for chemistry

The material is a metal-organic framework, a family chemists know well under its initials, MOF: metal ions serving as joints and organic molecules serving as struts, joined in repeating order until the two form crystals full of cavities, an orderly emptiness at molecular scale.

The human history is recent and decorated. In 1989 Richard Robson combined copper ions with a four-armed molecule and produced a spacious, well-ordered crystal. In the 1990s Omar Yaghi and Susumu Kitagawa turned such constructions into a general method, materials that could be designed pore by pore. More than 100,000 structures followed. In 2025 the three received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of metal-organic frameworks.34

The celebrated virtue of these materials is storage. A MOF can hold gases in its cavities, pull water from desert air, trap carbon dioxide, keep toxic compounds contained. A sugar-cube amount, unfolded, can present the surface area of a football field. The human framework is a room, built to be filled.3

The bacterial framework is a wire, built to be crossed: its metal is nickel, its linkers are organic dithiolene ligands, and although the building blocks match the ones chemists use, as Meysman notes, the assembly does not. The bacterium weaves its framework into bundles of intertwined nanoribbons, an architecture the Antwerp team describes as unseen in human-made materials. Conductivity approaches that of metals. It exceeds most synthetic MOFs and stands comparison with the best synthetic polymers.2

Earlier by some margin

Cable bacteria capable of building conductive structures originated about 600 million years ago, on Meysman's account. That date sits before land plants. It sits before forests, before flowers, before any animal with a backbone swam. For most of the time since, these filaments have stood in dark sediment on every sampled coast, oxidising sulfide at one end and reducing oxygen at the other, running a current through a framework no one had a name for.2

The signature appears to be shared: the same spectroscopic fingerprint was detected across cable bacteria from different environments, and some 90 species in the family employ conductive structures that may all depend on frameworks of this kind. One species supplied the identification, but a whole family may have been running the same electrical arrangement, in mud, worldwide, while chemistry above the mudline congratulated itself on a new form of molecular architecture.2

Priority is the least interesting part of this. Robson, Kitagawa and Yaghi did real work: they made the framework a method, something a chemist could specify in advance and vary at will. The bacterium makes one framework, for one purpose, and cannot be consulted about it. What the bacterium changes is the category. A MOF was, by definition, a synthetic material, a thing laboratories make. The definition held because no one had sectioned a bacterial wire and looked closely enough. Categories in science often have that structure. They describe the search as much as the world.

The current continues

The Antwerp group is now investigating how to produce the bacterial wires, and preparing a start-up to commercialise the material for sustainable electronics. The direction of instruction has reversed. A filament that cannot be interviewed will be reverse-engineered, its ribbon architecture copied into materials designed on purpose. Chemists spent three decades learning to build frameworks for their emptiness. The older practitioner used the same kit to build for movement instead, electrons in transit rather than molecules at rest.2

None of this registers in the sediment, where the top of a filament takes oxygen from the water, the bottom gives up electrons from sulfide, and between them, through nickel joints and organic struts woven into ribbons, the current moves over centimetres of dark. It was moving before the framework had a name, before the name had a prize, before there was a surface world equipped to notice. The mud was never still. It was wired.
