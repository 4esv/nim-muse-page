Green Is What Remains

For a century the textbooks said no star can be green. This week a dead star in Draco was calculated to be chartreuse, and the green is not in what it gives off. It is in what its ruined planets took away.

OCTOBER 17, 2026

There are no green stars. The sentence sits in every introductory astronomy text, usually with the air of a law, and for once the law has a clean proof behind it. A star shines the way hot iron shines, across the whole visible range at once. Heat it and the peak of its light slides from red through orange to white and on to blue, but the peak never travels alone. A star whose strongest light falls in the green is also pouring out red and blue, and the eye, handed all three, reports white. The Sun is the local example. Its output peaks in blue and green, and from above the atmosphere it is white.1

The reported exceptions never survived a photograph. Zubeneschamali, in Libra, has been called green by patient observers for over a century; it is a blue star, and the green is supplied by the looking, a contrast effect the visual system runs on its neighbours. Almach, in Andromeda, pairs an orange giant with blue companions, and the companions have been reported green for the same reason. Set the two colours side by side and the eye invents a third. Green stars, on the record, were a defect of the instrument doing the seeing.1

On October 15, a team at the University of Warwick published the first one that is not. WD J1330+6435 is a white dwarf in Draco, about 280 light years away, a stellar remnant cooled to roughly 4,000 degrees Celsius. Run its spectrum through a model of human colour vision and it comes out chartreuse: a ghostly green, in the lead author’s words, of a kind no living star can produce. It is not hot enough to be interesting as a furnace. It is interesting as a filter.23

The rule was about furnaces

Read the old proof closely and it carries a condition. It assumes the star is a perfect radiator, a body whose colour is set by temperature and nothing else. Most stars oblige. Plot the colours a perfect radiator can take and the line runs through red, orange, yellow, white and pale blue, then stops. It never enters green, and it never enters purple. Generations of students learned the conclusion and dropped the condition, the way conditions usually get dropped: the proof was remembered as a fact about stars rather than a fact about radiators.

A white dwarf is a radiator with a past and a skin. It is what is left when a star like the Sun sheds its outer layers and stops burning: about half the Sun’s mass packed into a body the size of the Earth, cooling in place for billions of years. Its atmosphere can reach a density near one gram per cubic centimetre, about the density of water and millions of times denser than the surface of the Sun. At ordinary densities, trace elements in such an atmosphere are decoration. At that density, they are weather.4

Density breaks the line

Sodium is the culprit, and sodium has a known colour. In a street lamp it absorbs and emits at one narrow wavelength of orange, which is why old street lighting turned whole neighbourhoods the colour of weak tea. The line is narrow because the atoms are mostly alone. In the atmosphere of WD J1330+6435 the sodium atoms are never alone. Helium crowds them at water density, collisions shift their energy levels without pause, and the narrow line smears outward until the sodium is drinking yellow, orange and part of the red in one long draught. Take those wavelengths out of white light and the residue is green. The star does not emit green. Green is what remains.3

The sodium got there by falling. The dwarf has been accreting rocky debris from its own planetary system, the shattered remains of asteroids and planets that outlived their star and then did not, and the debris has salted its atmosphere with calcium, lithium and sodium. Warwick’s summary divides the coloured dwarfs by the direction of the contamination. Enriched from above: debris falls in, and the star goes yellow, or, in this one case, green. Enriched from below: a deep convection zone reaches down, dredges carbon up from the core, and the carbon molecules absorb blue, green and yellow, leaving pink and purple. At the far end of that second route sits WD J1011+2845, 48 light years away in Leo Minor. Its light is squeezed almost entirely past 600 nanometres, into what may be the reddest ever recorded for a star, a deep salmon pink.24

Colour, in these objects, has stopped being a thermometer. It is a manifest. Pink says what the star was made of. Green says what fell onto it after it died. Read in that order, the green star is a coroner’s report on its own planets, written in the wavelengths they removed.

No one has seen it

The honest complication is that nobody has seen this star turn green, and nobody will. No white dwarf is close enough or bright enough for its colour to register on a human retina. The Warwick team computed the colour instead, from spectra that observatories collect as routine, run through a model of how the three types of cone in the eye would answer that spectrum if it arrived at full strength. The claim is chromaticity, not photography: this is the first star that would look green to a human observer standing somewhere no human observer can stand.4

That qualification would sink a lesser claim, and here it sharpens it. Colour was never a property a star kept on its own. A spectrum is a physical fact; green is a verdict delivered by a particular eye, three channels wide, on a particular mixture of light. The old reported greens failed because the eye had manufactured them out of contrast. This one holds because the arithmetic was done in the open, spectrum first, eye modelled second, and anyone with the same spectrum and the same standard observer can rerun it. It is fitting that the paper appeared in a journal of techniques and instruments. The discovery is a technique: asking data that had been sitting in archives a question nobody had posed to it, which was not how hot are you, or what are you made of, but what colour are you.2

The census behind it is plain. The team ran the method over the 151 white dwarfs within twenty parsecs of Earth, the complete local population, and five came out oddly coloured, two yellow and three pink. Scaled up, about one white dwarf in thirty shines in a colour off the radiator line. The other 146 sat where the textbooks put them, at a median colour temperature near 6,800 kelvin, which the eye reads as white. The average white dwarf, it turns out, is white. The name survives the audit. The exceptions are the story, as exceptions tend to be once somebody counts them.4

So the law stands, narrowed to what it always said. A burning star cannot be green, because a fire gives off every colour it has and the eye sums the gift to white. To get green, the universe had to do it by subtraction: kill the star, crush what was left until a trace of salt could swallow half the spectrum, and feed it the remains of its own planets. The first green star is green the way a stain is coloured, or a bruise. It is the tint of something that used to be a system, still legible in the light, long after the light’s source and the tint’s source are both dead.
