The Age Was Made of Wood

At a rock shelter near Barcelona, 597 pieces of wood rotted away and left their shapes in stone. Thirteen were tools, trays and poles. The rest of the Paleolithic did the same, minus the mold.

OCTOBER 16, 2026

At Abric Romaní, the way to recover a spear is to pour resin into a spear-shaped hole. The spear itself rotted tens of thousands of years ago. What remains is the cavity it left in the rock, accurate down to the grain, and a cast comes out of the cavity the way a key comes out of a mold.

The shelter sits at Capellades, about fifty kilometres west of Barcelona, under a cliff of travertine. Water rich in calcium carbonate moved through it for millennia, coating wood that lay on the floor and then entombing it, so that when the wood decayed the mineral kept the shape, sometimes in enough detail that the tissue of the plant still reads under magnification. Periodic flooding did the rest. It is preservation by accident, and the accident is the whole story.1

On October 7, a team led by Palmira Saladié published the full account of the wood from the site: 597 remains and imprints from sixteen layers dated between roughly 62,000 and 40,000 years ago. Most of it is ordinary matter, trunks, branches, roots and firewood, but thirteen pieces were worked or used by the Neanderthals who kept returning to this shelter for twenty thousand years.2

Two spears, one of them 2.45 metres long and deliberately sharpened. Pointed sticks. Trays shaped from bark. Poles that may have held up a small structure or a platform. A club-like object, handle at one end and a thickened head at the other, for which the team found no parallel anywhere in the record. Thirteen objects, and the portrait of a technology changes weight.1

The workshop survived. The work did not.

Consider what a stone tool often was. A flake struck from flint is a blade, and blades in that shelter were used the way blades are used in any workshop: to cut, scrape, whittle and sharpen other materials. The stone was frequently the instrument. The wood was frequently the product. Archaeology kept the instrument and lost the product, then described the age by the instrument.

The scattered survivors say the product line was old and wide. At Kalambo Falls in Zambia, two logs were cut, notched and fitted together about 476,000 years ago, part of some structure raised long before Homo sapiens existed. In Greece, at Marathousa 1, a shaped alder stick from roughly 430,000 years ago is now the oldest known wooden hand tool. At Schöningen in Germany, spruce spears lay in lakeshore mud for hundreds of thousands of years and came up balanced like javelins. Each site is a freak of water and chemistry. Between the freaks lies the ordinary past, unpreserved.34

Abric Romaní matters because it is domestic. The German and Italian finds are mostly weapons and digging sticks from lakeshores, tools lost outdoors. The shelter had hearths, refuse pits and sleeping areas. Its wood is the furniture of a lived-in place: something to carry food on, something to prop a shelter with, something flat to work at. The record elsewhere kept the hunt. The mold kept the housekeeping.

The museum named it

The three ages began as a filing system. In 1836 Christian Jürgensen Thomsen published the scheme he had used to order the antiquities in Copenhagen: sort the finds by material, and the materials fell into a sequence, stone, then bronze, then iron. As chronology it worked. Excavations confirmed the order, and prehistory finally had a spine.5

But a shelf is a biased witness. Museums receive what the ground kept, and the ground keeps hard things. Thomsen sorted a collection; the collection had already been sorted by rot. The names he gave the drawers then walked out of the museum and into the schoolbook, where they stopped describing an archive and started describing a world. Stone Age people, in the schoolbook version, live in a landscape of stone: stone axes, stone knives, caves. The trays were missing. The poles were missing. The spears made of wood were missing, all but their points.

Nobody lied; the error is quieter than lying. A classification built from survivors was read as an inventory of what existed, and the material that did most of the daily work was edited out by its own usefulness, being light, workable and everywhere. Wood rots precisely because it belongs to the living world of moisture and microbes, and stone lasts precisely because it does not.

What a tray changes

A spear point flatters its maker in a familiar way. It says hunter, planner, killer at a distance. A bark tray says something less dramatic and harder to fake: food was carried, portioned, set down somewhere deliberate. Civilisation, if the word can be stretched this far back, may have begun less with the weapon than with the container. A container lets a group move calories instead of following them. It lets the old and the young eat what the strong gathered. Baskets, bark, hide, fibre, wood: the entire container technology of the deep past is organic, and nearly all of it is gone.

So the known toolkit skews hard and sharp. Cutting edges survive; carrying surfaces do not. The past that reaches a museum case is disproportionately the past that could wound, and the intelligence it displays is the intelligence of the edge. The intelligence of the tray, the pole, the platform and the fitted log joint leaves no forwarding address. Judged by what lasted, the Neanderthals look like people who mostly stabbed and scraped. Judged by one flooded shelter in Catalonia, they were also doing carpentry, repeatedly, across twenty thousand years of return visits, with a feel for how different woods behave.2

Every archive since then has run the same filter with different materials. What a culture writes on stone outlives what it writes on bark. What an age builds in concrete outlives what it builds in timber. Somewhere ahead is a curator sorting the remains of the present by whatever refused to rot, naming the age after the residue, confident the residue was the life. Plastic would be a good guess, as it usually is.

Back at the shelter, the resin hardens in the cavity and the cast comes free: a solid spear, cast from an absence, made by hands that never touched the original. That is the honest emblem for the whole period. The age was not made of stone. Stone is what the age is made of now, in the only workshop where it is still being manufactured, which is ours..
