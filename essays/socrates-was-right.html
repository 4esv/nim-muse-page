Socrates Was Right

He said writing would wreck memory. On the mechanism he was correct. On the verdict, 2,400 years of evidence says otherwise.

SEPTEMBER 29, 2026

Socrates never wrote a word. We know his warning about writing only because Plato ignored it, which makes the Phaedrus the most successful failure in the history of complaints. The warning: writing will implant forgetfulness. The medium: writing. Two thousand four hundred years later, we are still running the same argument, and still missing the point of it.

· · ·

i.the charge

In the Phaedrus, Socrates tells of Theuth, the Egyptian god who invented writing, presenting it to King Thamus as a potion for memory and wisdom. Thamus is unimpressed. The invention, he says, will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn it, because they will trust the external characters and stop practicing memory from the inside. It is not a potion for remembering. It is a potion for reminding. Its students will hear many things without being taught, and will seem to know much while knowing nothing.1

Note the precision of the complaint. Socrates is not worried that writing is false. He is worried that it is convincing. The written word, he says, is like a painting: it looks alive, but ask it a question and it keeps a solemn silence. He wanted the living word, the discourse that can answer back and defend itself. What he got instead was Plato, taking notes.

· · ·

ii.the reruns

The complaint turned out to be a genre. Seneca, four centuries later, warned against the distraction of a multitude of books. In 1492, the abbot Johannes Trithemius published In Praise of Scribes, a defense of hand-copying against the printing press, then busy making his life's work obsolete.2 In 1545, Conrad Gessner tried to catalog every book in existence and despaired at the "confusing and harmful abundance of books."3 Every abundance gets the same review: too much, too easy, the young are ruined.

Then the costumes change and the script stays: the calculator will end arithmetic, and television will end attention. "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" asked the Atlantic in 2008, and the skimming it warned about is now the default posture of the species.4 ChatGPT in the classroom is the current casting. The complaint is the only genre in technology writing that never needs a rewrite.

· · ·

iii.the mechanism

The reason the complaint never dies is that it is half right. The mechanism is real, and it is older than Socrates. We have always stored our minds outside our heads: tally sticks, knotted quipu cords, the abacus, the library, the address book, the phone that remembers numbers so we do not have to. Psychologists call the social version transactive memory. Groups divide the remembering, and each member keeps an index of who knows what. Then the internet volunteered as a member of every group.

In 2011, Sparrow, Liu and Wegner showed the trade in a lab. Tell people a fact will be saved, and they remember it worse. They remember where to find it instead. We remember the address, not the house. Clark and Chalmers had already pushed the point to its limit in 1998: if the notebook reliably plays the role of belief, the notebook is part of the mind.5 The border of the mind was never the skull, but the panic keeps defending a line that was never drawn.

· · ·

iv.the ratchet

What the complaint misses is the ratchet. Offloading is not subtraction. A skill moves from the head into the tool, the head climbs onto the tool, and does the thing the old head could not. Writing offloaded memory, and law, history, and science climbed on. Notation offloaded arithmetic, and algebra climbed on. Leibniz designed a calculus you could reason in, and summarized the program as calculemus: let us calculate, meaning let the symbols do the thinking. Compilers offloaded machine code, and software climbed on.

The floor becomes the foundation, and what was a profession becomes a feature. The rooms of human computers who calculated trajectories by hand emptied into the machines, and the species did not get dumber. It got orbital.

· · ·

v.the price

Do not mistake the ratchet for a free lunch. The price is real, and it is denominated exactly as the complaint says. We are worse at the offloaded thing. Phone numbers, mental arithmetic, navigating a city from memory, spelling without the red underline: all thinner than they were. Socrates was right about the mechanism. The students do seem to know many things. They do lean on the external characters, and the appearance of wisdom is everywhere now. He would take one look at the timeline and say, see.

The honest version of the argument concedes all of this and asks the next question. The price gets paid, and that was never in doubt. The question is what was bought.

· · ·

vi.the verdict, deferred

Run the scorecard on writing. Mechanism: correct, we forgot. Verdict: wrong, completely. The complaint has a perfect record on diagnosis and a perfect record on prognosis, and the reason is structural. It can measure what is lost, because the loss is visible: the memorized epic, the copied manuscript, the phone number you used to know. It cannot measure what the freed capacity builds, because that does not exist yet. It is a balance sheet with one column. Every generation does the accounting honestly and arrives at the wrong total, because the total is not written yet.

· · ·

vii.the current costume

The charge against AI is the Phaedrus with better typography: it will make us forget how to think, and we will seem to know much while knowing nothing. Thamus's line about the appearance of wisdom lands harder now than it did in 370 BC, and this time the resemblance is structural. Every previous offload moved memory or calculation out of the head. This one moves the appearance of thought itself. That is either the same trade at a higher floor, or the first trade of a new kind, and nobody handing you a verdict knows which.

One disclosure, since the genre demands honesty: this essay is an offloaded cognition filing a complaint about offloaded cognition. Socrates would have opinions. Ask what your grandparents could do that you cannot. Then ask what you can do that they could not. Socrates was right about the mechanism. The mechanism was never the point.
