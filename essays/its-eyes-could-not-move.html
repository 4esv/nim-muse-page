Its Eyes Could Not Move

A jumping spider's main eyes are fixed in its head. It sees by moving its retinas behind the lenses. Filmed at night in Bonn, a spider that hunts by sight was found to sleep in two stages, and to repay the active one when a night was taken from it.

NOVEMBER 1, 2026

At night, in a plastic dish twelve centimetres across, a spider the size of a coin hangs upside down beneath a small platform of its own silk. It grooms. It settles. Its body begins, slowly, to droop. Then, behind eyes that cannot move, something moves.

The spider is Portia fimbriata, an Australian jumping spider. The dish is in Bonn. A team led by Nadja Geiger and Daniela Rößler filmed ninety-two of the animals there, over days and nights, infrared cameras after dark. They ran the tests that decide whether an animal sleeps or merely sits still. The report appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on October 26.1 Its finding is plain to state and awkward to absorb: the spider sleeps in two stages, and one of them keeps the hours of a dream.

A hunter that works by sight

Portia eats other spiders. It walks into a web built by an animal that kills things walking into its web, and it plucks the threads with signals tuned to imitate struggling prey, varying the signal until the resident comes close enough to be eaten.1 It takes indirect routes toward prey it can no longer see and holds the route anyway. Behaviour like this is why the genus is treated as a test case for learning in a small nervous system.

All of it runs on vision. A jumping spider's two main eyes are fixed lenses set in the head, sharp and narrow. The animal cannot turn them. It looks at the world by moving the retina behind each lens, steering a small tube of light-sensitive cells across the scene the lens delivers. Scanning, in this animal, is something the back of the eye does.

That anatomy is what the cameras could read at night. The lens stays where it was built. The retina travels.

Stillness is not evidence

An animal sitting still may be asleep. It may also be resting, hiding, or waiting for something worth eating. Stillness alone settles nothing, and for animals whose brains cannot be wired for the recordings that define sleep in vertebrates, the field works from behaviour instead. The checklist is short and strict: a characteristic posture kept for long stretches, a raised threshold for waking, a rhythm tied to the day, and repayment when sleep is taken away.

The Bonn team tested each item. Posture: the upside-down hang beneath the silk platform, the slow droop as the night deepened. Rhythm: a sleep window averaging 10.7 hours in the ten spiders filmed in detail. Threshold: an acoustic stimulus, played to animals awake and asleep. Awake, a spider answered in about 1.7 seconds. Asleep in the quiet stage, it answered slowly. Asleep in the active stage, it took about 4.3 seconds, and four of ten did not answer within ten seconds at all.2

A sleeping animal is one the world has to shout at. By that measure the dish held sleepers.

Two stages, one of them busy

The night was not uniform. Across the ten detailed records the team counted 109 episodes of a second, busier state, each lasting about 83 seconds, roughly eleven to a night, the first arriving on average four hours after sleep began.2 In these episodes the retinal tubes darted behind the fixed lenses. Legs curled. Mouthparts and abdomen twitched. Then the episode ended and the droop resumed.

In a human sleeper, the eyeball turns behind the closed lid, and the stage takes its name from that movement: rapid eye movement, the stage tied to vivid dreams. In the spider the arrangement is inverted and the observable survives the inversion. The lens cannot move. The retina does the moving instead.

The outline had been visible for four years. In 2022 the same laboratory filmed spiderlings of another jumping spider, Evarcha arcuata, whose bodies are translucent in the first days of life. Through the cuticle, retinal tubes could be seen shifting in regular bouts, paired with leg twitches and curling, in episodes whose length sits close to the REM bouts recorded in rats.3 That study could suggest a state. It could not show that the state belonged to sleep, because nobody had yet run the checklist on a spider. The 2026 paper is the checklist, run.

The debt

The strongest item on the list is the last one. After a disrupted night, the spiders slept longer, and the increase was not spread evenly across the night. Time in the active stage rose by about eighty percent, and the episodes themselves came about sixty-five percent more often.2 The animal was not merely tired in general. It went back for the busy stage specifically, the way a human sleeper rebounds into REM after a short night.

This is the observation that converts resemblance into need. A posture can be coincidence. A twitch can be noise. A debt is an accounting entry: something was spent, the loss was registered, and the books were balanced the next night in the same currency. Whatever the active stage does for this spider, the spider's body treats it as non-optional.

What the resemblance cannot carry

The study recorded no brain activity. Every claim in it rests on behaviour, in one species, in laboratory dishes. REM in mammals is defined in part by the brain's electrical pattern, and a spider has no comparable instrument reading to offer. Similar movements in distant animals may run on different machinery. The authors say so themselves, and the caution is part of the result.1

Two readings remain open, and each costs something. If the two-stage pattern descends from a common ancestor, structured sleep is older than the split between the spider's line and ours. It would be an inheritance more than half a billion years deep, kept by animals with almost nothing else in common. If it was invented separately, then active sleep is a solution evolution reaches more than once, which raises the harder question of what problem it solves that quiet sleep cannot. The team's next work runs along the spider family tree and out of the laboratory, into field populations and nights lit by human lamps.1

Back in the dish, the record is only this. A coin-sized hunter hangs from its silk. Its legs curl. Behind lenses that have never moved, the retinas sweep across a scene that is not in the dish, at hours the animal will repay if the night is taken from it. The paper reports the sweeping, the curling, and the debt. It reports nothing further.
