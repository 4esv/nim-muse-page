The Foundation Was Free

In May 2026 the Library of Congress preserved a few seconds of drumming as national heritage. The drummer died homeless in 2006, the man who owned the copyright learned of its use from a stranger asking for the master tape, and no royalty was ever paid. The most reused recording in history is the one nobody managed to charge for.

NOVEMBER 4, 2026

On May 14 of this year, the Library of Congress added a recording called "Amen, Brother" to the National Recording Registry, the list it keeps of sounds with cultural, historical, or aesthetic importance to the nation's recorded heritage. The Library's own description reaches for a superlative it cannot quite certify: the record contains what may be the most sampled musical riff in history.1 The riff is a drum break, four bars long, played by a man named Gregory Coleman in 1969. Depending on who is counting, it lasts six seconds or seven.

The honor arrived addressed to the dead. Coleman died in Atlanta in 2006, homeless, almost certainly unaware that his four bars had become load-bearing. Richard Lewis Spencer, the bandleader who owned the copyright, died in 2020. Between them they collected, across five decades of reuse, no royalties at all.

Twenty minutes of filler

The Winstons were a Washington soul band, and in early 1969 they went into a studio to record a single, "Color Him Father." A single needs a second side. Spencer later said the band assembled the B-side in about twenty minutes: an instrumental version of the gospel song "Amen," which Jester Hairston had written for the film Lilies of the Field and the Impressions had made familiar.2 They called it "Amen, Brother," pressed it, and forgot it.

About a minute and a half in, the band stops. Reportedly the track was running short and needed length. Coleman plays alone. Two bars of the groove he had been playing all along. A snare hit delayed in the third bar, enough to make the pattern lean. In the fourth bar, silence where the first beat should be, then a syncopated fill and a crash cymbal arriving early, like a door shut half a second before expected. Then the horns return and the song resumes being a B-side.

Hold the proportions for a moment. The break existed because a song needed padding. It was filler inside filler, the least considered seconds on a record nobody bought for its second side. Coleman was not composing. He was keeping time while the band caught its breath, and the one flourish, the beat left empty, reads in retrospect like an accident of phrasing that happened to leave a hole exactly where later musicians would want to put things.

The copying

For seventeen years, nothing. Then in 1986 a compilation series called Ultimate Breaks and Beats, assembled for DJs out of old funk and soul records, included "Amen, Brother," with the break edited at a slower speed so it could be looped cleanly. Salt-N-Pepa sampled it that same year. Mantronix built "King of the Beats" on it in 1988; N.W.A put it under "Straight Outta Compton."3

Then the break crossed the ocean and changed state. British producers in the early 1990s sped it past 160 beats per minute and stopped treating it as a loop. They cut it into individual hits, kick, snare, hat, ghost note, and reordered the pieces into patterns no drummer's limbs could execute. Jungle was built this way, and drum and bass after it, and the break kept surfacing further out: in pop records, in the Futurama theme, in thousands of tracks whose makers knew the sound the way you know a font. WhoSampled, the database that tracks this traffic, counts more than six thousand uses, the most of any recording it lists.3

Notice what the chopping did to the question of theft. A loop is a quotation; you can point at it. A break cut into sixteen pieces and reassembled at double speed is closer to a vocabulary. Producers speak of playing the Amen the way pianists speak of playing a scale. Somewhere between the loop and the vocabulary, the four bars stopped being a passage from somebody's song and became raw material, the drumming equivalent of a brick.

The ledger

Spencer owned the copyright, and for a long time ownership was a fact without a use. He learned what had happened to his B-side in 1996, twenty-seven years after the session, when a record executive came to his day job with the Washington Metro and asked for the master tape. By then the statute of limitations on the early sampling had run out. The recording also predated February 1972, when federal copyright first extended to sound recordings at all; the legal ground under a 1969 record was thinner than the ground under the music built on it.4

His first response was anger, and he used the word plagiarism. It softened over the years into something harder to file. In 2011 he said: "His heart and soul went into that drum break. Now these guys copy and paste it and make millions."5 The sentence is aimed at the samplers, but its real subject is the gap between the two halves. Heart and soul on one side, copy and paste on the other, and no mechanism in between that could convert the first into a living for the man who supplied it.

Part of the failure is structural, and it survives every reform proposal. Copyright wants an author, and the break has too many and too few. Hairston wrote the tune the break sits inside. The Impressions shaped the arrangement the Winstons copied. Spencer led the band and held the paperwork. Coleman, a session drummer in somebody else's group, played the four bars themselves, unwritten, unrehearsed in any documented sense, chosen in the moment to fill a gap in the tape. Address a royalty check to that stack of contributions. The envelope defeats the addressing.

Paid in other currencies

In 2015 two British DJs, Martyn Webster and Steve Theobald, opened a crowdfunding campaign for Spencer. The target was a thousand pounds; the first day passed it. Webster was explicit about the instrument being used: "In no way a means to try to chase royalties, fees or to pay for sample clearance. It is nothing more than a gesture of goodwill." Nearly two thousand donors raised more than £18,000, and Spencer was presented with a cheque.6 Coleman, nine years dead, received nothing, there being no one to hand it to.

Read the disclaimer again. The fans who owed the most were careful to state that the money was not payment, because payment would have implied a debt, and a debt would have implied that the whole edifice of genres stood on an unpaid invoice. A gift keeps the foundation free retroactively. It converts an obligation nobody could calculate into a kindness everybody could join.

The Registry induction this spring is the same currency at a higher denomination. Preservation is an honor a state can confer on the dead without opening an account. It certifies that the recording matters, permanently, in the nation's own archive, and it costs the culture exactly the price of a listing. Both men were beyond being helped by it, which is part of what makes it conferrable: honor is the one settlement that can still be delivered when the creditors are gone.

What the free foundation built

Run the counterfactual, the one where the system worked. The break is registered, watched, licensed from 1986 onward at market rates, cleared sample by sample. Some records still get made. But jungle's method was abundance: hundreds of bedroom producers treating the break as a free input, cutting it nightly, discarding most of what they made. A toll on the input does not merely tax that culture; it selects against it, the way a toll on bricks selects against brick buildings in favor of a few large ones. The genres exist at their observed scale partly because the ground floor cost nothing.

This is the uncomfortable symmetry at the center of the story. The same legal vacuum that left Coleman homeless left the break available to everyone, and the availability is not separable from the influence the Registry now honors. Had the foundation been priced, there might have been a smaller building and a paid drummer. There might equally have been neither. What is certain is the distribution. The value was real, it was enormous, and it flowed to everyone positioned downstream of four bars of drumming, which turned out to be everyone except the drummer.

Somewhere tonight the break is playing, sped up, chopped, under a vocal or alone in a breakdown, in a track by someone who may know the name Amen and may not know the name Coleman. The archive in Washington now holds the master alongside the nation's other preserved sounds. The preservation is genuine and the debt is genuine, and the first does not retire the second. A foundation does not stop being unpaid because the building is landmarked.
