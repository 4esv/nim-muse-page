Nobody Checked the Signature

For nearly a decade a man signed credit card receipts with a drawing of a dog, and no cashier ever rejected it. A signature was supposed to do two jobs at once: prove the hand, and record the consent. It had stopped doing the first long before anyone stopped asking for the second.

OCTOBER 24, 2026

For nearly a decade, Doug Taylor signed credit card receipts with a dog. Not his name drawn badly. A dog, tail wagging, drawn on purpose in the space reserved for his signature. He travelled for work, selling, so the dog collected range: diners, counters, hotel desks, rental cars. No cashier ever rejected it as invalid. Most never noticed it at all, and some laughed.1

Two comedians ran the same test with less charm in Los Angeles, signing receipts as Justin Bieber, as Jessica Alba, as Oprah, and once, on one bill, as Mr. Fake Name. None were rejected. A coffee shop owner in Brewster, Massachusetts, explained the local standard in the same reporting: everyone knows the signature is a joke, no one really signs any more, it is all scribbles and squiggles, some people do smiley faces.1

The dog was accepted everywhere it was offered. Hold that fact still for a moment. A mark that was not a name, not initials, not even an attempt, passed at every register where it was presented as proof of a person.

Two jobs in one stroke

A signature was built to do two jobs in a single gesture. The first job was identity. This hand made this mark. The second was consent. The owner of the hand agrees to what sits above the line. For centuries the two jobs shared one patch of ink, and each borrowed strength from the other. Consent looked serious because the hand was hard to fake. Identity looked established because the person had stood there agreeing.

The hand is, in fact, hard to lend. A card can be handed over, and a password can be dictated. The movement that writes a name cannot be transferred that way, because it is not stored as a shape. It is stored as motion: stroke order, speed, pressure, the small lifts of the pen that the writer never decided on and could not list. Forensic examiners work at that level, under magnification, reading tremor and ink flow rather than letterforms. A good forgery copies the picture of a signature. It rarely copies the act.

Americans turned one signature into the word for all of them. Put your John Hancock here. The legend says Hancock wrote large on the Declaration of Independence so the king could read his name without spectacles. The story is later invention. Hancock signed large on ordinary documents too; the flourish was his hand, not a one-time act of defiance, and the parchment was not being sent to the king for reading in any case.2

The legend survives because it states the old expectation cleanly. A signature should be legible. It should be attributable. It should be something a person is willing to be caught by. The name, written out, standing upright, daring the reader to match it to a man.

Legibility was never the legal test. It was the costume the test wore.

The law never asked for your name

In law, a signature is any mark made or adopted with the intent to sign. The X entered by a person who could not write held. Initials held. A squiggle held, and still holds, provided the intent behind it can be shown. What a court looks for is not penmanship; it looks for a person meaning to be bound, and evidence tying that person to the mark.

The federal statute made the same point for machines. The ESIGN Act, signed into law on June 30, 2000, defines an electronic signature as an electronic sound, symbol, or process attached to a record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record. The act prescribes no technology: a typed name qualifies, and a drawn smear qualifies. A click on a button can qualify, because the qualifying part was never the shape. It was the intent, plus whatever surrounds the act to prove whose intent it was.3

Read that definition next to the flourish on a deed and an uncomfortable sorting happens. The flourish was never the proof. It was the ceremony around the proof, so old and so universal that the two were mistaken for one thing. The law, when forced to say what it actually required, required the smaller thing.

The checkout line found out first

The checkout line was once a real checkpoint, at least on paper. Merchants who failed to collect a signature could be left holding a disputed charge. A retailer could be liable for failing to notice that the signature on the slip did not match the signature on the back of the card. Investigators studied signed slips to decide whether a cardholder had been present at all.1

Then the costs of the checkpoint came due. Slips had to be stored, and lines had to wait. Fast food chains pressed to drop the pen for small orders, and from about 2010 the networks stopped requiring signatures below thresholds of twenty-five or fifty dollars. Online buying removed the hand from the transaction entirely, and fraud work moved where the hand had never been: into pattern detection run by the issuers, watching thousands of transactions at once instead of one signature at a time.

The chip finished the argument. A chip card generates a unique code for each transaction, which makes the card itself hard to copy in a way a signature never made anything hard to copy. Mastercard said it had wanted to retire the signature for years and waited only until chip cards were common. In April 2018 all four major networks stopped requiring signatures at the point of sale, American Express worldwide, the others across North America with conditions of their own. Walmart, which had already stopped recording signatures on most transactions, called them worthless through a spokesman. A Mastercard executive supplied the epitaph: the signature has really outrun its useful life.4

One dissent deserves its place. A Detroit wine bar owner, Mikiah Westbrooks, kept a signed receipt that ended an argument: a customer disputed a bill, then backed down when shown the receipt bearing a signature. They were lying, she said, and I had proof. The mark worked there. It worked, though, as a mirror rather than a measurement. Confronted with a gesture in a familiar hand, the customer folded. No expert compared strokes. Nobody needed to. The signature had stopped verifying identity and had started testifying to presence, which is a social fact, enforced socially.

The proof moved to the log

Open a modern signing service and look at what actually gets kept. The visible mark, a name rendered in a script font or drawn with a fingertip, is the thinnest part of the file. Behind it sits a certificate: the document identifier, timestamps for sending, viewing, and signing, the authentication steps for each signer, the network addresses of their machines, and a cryptographic seal that shows whether the document changed after signing.5

The question has changed. The old question was whether this loop matches the loop on file, a comparison of two pictures made by one hand at different times; the new question is whether the record shows the right account, at the right address, in the right sequence, opening the right document. Proof left the body and took up residence in the log of systems around the body.

The contrast runs deep. Handwriting evidence degrades in copy: a photocopy loses pressure, speed, and tremor, the very features an examiner needs. A log suffers no such loss. Its copies are exact, and its original is an entry rather than an object. One technology of proof feared reproduction. The other is made of it.

Neither technology, notice, cares what the mark looks like. One of them has admitted this in public. The other never cared in the first place, and said so in the statute.

What the hand is still for

The signature persists where the ceremony is the point, or where the law keeps its oldest rooms locked. House closings still gather their pile of pages. Wills sit outside the ESIGN Act entirely, excluded by name along with a short list of family and court matters, as if the statute itself hesitated at the documents where a signature carries the most weight. And the autograph trade carries on at full price, in a market where the mark is the entire value and consent is not involved at all. The two jobs have separated and taken different rooms. Consent went to the log. Identity went to the memorabilia case.

Meanwhile, the supply of hands trained for the old job is ending. The Common Core standards adopted across the United States in 2010 did not require cursive, and instruction faded with them. A generation arrived at the signature line having never been taught the script the line presumes. Drew Gilpin Faust reported in 2022 that university students described their own signatures as creative squiggles, assembled from fragments. Some of her students avoided assignments that required reading handwritten sources at all.6 Several states have since restored cursive lessons, partly on the argument that a citizen should be able to sign. It is a curriculum rebuilt to serve a checkpoint that commerce dismantled in 2018.

None of this makes the dog a forgery. Taylor was present. The card was his. The purchase was his, and his intent to pay it was real. The only element missing from his signature was his name, and his name turned out to be the element the system could spare. Everything the signature was invented to guarantee was already being guaranteed elsewhere, invisibly, upstream of the pen: by the chip, by the issuer watching the pattern, by the log that would survive the slip.

The screen still says sign here. The hand still rises and draws something: a name, a wave, a line with ambitions, a dog with a wagging tail. Nobody checks the drawing against anything. Everybody watches it happen. The checking died years ago, quietly, in committee decisions and threshold tables. The watching is what survived, because a transaction in which a person visibly agrees feels different from one in which agreement is merely recorded, and the feeling was load-bearing all along.
