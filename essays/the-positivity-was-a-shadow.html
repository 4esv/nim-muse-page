The Positivity Was a Shadow

For seventy years the science of meaning scored words by feeling: good or bad, calm or excited, weak or strong. Counted by actual use, billions of tokens at a time, the axes move. Underneath sit power, danger and structure, and a bias not toward happiness but toward safety.

OCTOBER 30, 2026

In 1969, two psychologists proposed that human beings use positive words more often, and in greater variety, than negative ones. Jerry Boucher and Charles Osgood called it the Pollyanna hypothesis, after the orphan in the novel who plays the glad game, finding something to be pleased about in every misfortune. The claim sounded like a claim about temperament. A talkative species, incurably sunny, talking its way past the evidence.1

The evidence, when it finally arrived at scale, agreed. In 2015 a team at the University of Vermont scored some 100,000 words across 24 corpora in ten languages, using five million individual human ratings. Spanish leaned happiest, Chinese least, and every source leaned the same way: books, newsprint, subtitles, song lyrics, the war reporting of the New York Times. Positive words outnumbered negative ones everywhere they looked. The glad game appeared to be a property of language itself.1

This year the same laboratory, led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth, published the sequel in Science Advances, and the sequel retracts the moral while keeping the measurement. The positivity is real. The interpretation was a shadow on a wall: a flat projection of something with one more dimension than the picture showed. What language leans toward, counted properly, is safety.2

Three dials for feeling

The older framework came from Osgood himself. In the 1950s he and his colleagues asked people to rate concepts between opposed adjectives: good against bad, strong against weak, active against passive. Hundreds of ratings, reduced by factor analysis, kept collapsing onto the same three factors. Evaluation. Potency. Activity. A word, on this account, is a point in a three dimensional space of feeling, and meaning is where the point sits.2

Later workers renamed the dials valence, arousal and dominance, and the renamed version became load-bearing. Sentiment analysis, the machinery that scores text for mood in psychology papers, brand dashboards and moderation queues, rests largely on lexicons built in this frame: lists of words, each carrying its three scores, waiting to be counted. The frame had the great virtue of being measurable. It had a quieter property, visible only later. It was built by asking about words one at a time, in isolation, as entries in a list.

A list is a census of the possible. Every word appears once, the rare and the common side by side, each with an equal vote. Language in the wild holds no such election. A handful of words do most of the work and most words barely appear. The average of a dictionary is the average of a country where every citizen is counted once, and nobody is counted at the hour they speak.

Count the uses, not the words

The Vermont team separates the two populations with the linguist's old pair of terms. A type is a word as an entry: apple, once, in the lexicon. A token is a word as an event: every apple actually written or said. Their new paper re-examines first the types in a standard lexicon of more than 20,000 scored English words, then the tokens, billions of them, across books, news, Wikipedia, social media and transcribed talk radio.2 Weighting by use is the whole methodological turn. Frequency stops being a nuisance variable and becomes the terrain.

Weighted, the old geometry sags. The three feeling dials turn out not to be independent: they lean on each other, correlations the factor analysis of the 1950s had smoothed away at survey scale. Rotating the space to axes that do stand apart produces a different set of coordinates, arranged by the authors in a ring they call GPADS: goodness, power, aggression, danger, structure. Compressed to the minimum that still carries the meaning, three axes remain. Power, weak to powerful. Danger, safe to dangerous. Structure, ordered to chaotic.3

The rotated frame accounts for more than 90 percent of the variation in the measured meaning of the words, against roughly 72 percent for the feeling frame.3 Julia Zimmerman, a co-author, put the new axes plainly: power, danger and structure are relevant to every person that has ever lived.3 Read the three as questions and the plainness sharpens. How strong is it. Can it hurt me. Does it hold together. These are the questions an animal asks of anything it meets, in the order it asks them, long before it gets around to reporting how the meeting felt.

Positivity was the projection

Then the tokens deliver their own finding, separate from the geometry. Across the corpora, words on the safe end of the danger axis are used far more often than words on the dangerous end. The skew is systematic, it survives the change of medium, and it is stronger than a quirk of any one collection. Language, as practised, keeps its vocabulary of safety in heavy rotation and its vocabulary of danger in reserve.2

Set the two results side by side and the 1969 observation changes ownership. Safe things tend to be rated good. Dangerous things tend to be rated bad. So an axis of feeling, laid across a population that leans toward safety, registers a lean toward positivity, the way a shadow registers the outline of an object while discarding its depth. The paper states the demotion in one sentence: the Pollyanna principle's positivity bias is, in fact, a one-dimensional projection of an underlying safety bias.3 Dodds, the senior author, calls expressions of safety crucial to all language.3

The distinction survives contact with ordinary speech. Safe is not a synonym for pleasant. A safe meal can be a dull one, eaten without pleasure and remembered not at all. Dangerous is not a synonym for bad: people pay for danger in measured doses, on mountainsides and motorcycles, and describe the purchase in glowing terms afterwards. The two scales cross constantly in individual cases. In aggregate, across billions of uses, they align closely enough that fifty years of instruments pointed at one and recorded the other.

A warning label makes the crossing visible. Its vocabulary is almost entirely negative by the feeling lexicons: poison, injury, death, do not. Its function is the opposite of its mood. The text exists to keep a reader safe, and it does the work in the only register available, naming the danger precisely so the danger can be avoided. Score the label for sentiment and the score is grim. Score it on the danger axis and then ask what it is for, and the grimness resolves into care, written in the imperative.

Why safety would be underneath

The authors offer the obvious deep explanation and label it, correctly, as consistent with the data rather than proved by it. A communication system built by creatures who can be eaten, injured, frozen and lost will spend its capacity on the distinctions that decide those outcomes. Who is strong. What can harm. What is holding its shape and what is coming apart. Feeling, on this account, is the summary a mind files after the assessment is done, and the vocabulary kept the assessment and let the summary blur.2

The limits deserve the same size type as the claim. The rotated framework was established on English: one language, however large the sample, with its own history folded into its word stock. The safety skew in tokens is an English corpus result until other languages are run through the same rotation. The 2015 positivity result, by contrast, held across ten languages, which is suggestive and nothing more. A bias found everywhere in one measurement and deeply in one language in the other has an obvious next experiment attached to it, and the experiment has not been run.

There is also a circularity to keep in view. The new axes were recovered from human ratings of words, the same kind of evidence that produced the old ones. No instrument here touches meaning directly. What changed is the weighting, the rotation, and the willingness to let frequency speak. The finding is that one summary of the ratings survives contact with real usage and the other deforms. That is how instruments improve: not by escaping interpretation, but by failing less of it.

The machines learned the shadow

The timing gives the correction a second address. Sentiment analysis is no longer a research instrument sitting in a lab. It is plumbing: moderation systems triaging posts, dashboards pricing the mood of a market, evaluation suites grading the outputs of language models. A large share of that plumbing was laid in the feeling frame, scored from the same lexicon tradition the new paper rotates. If the frame confuses the report with the assessment, the plumbing inherits the confusion at industrial scale.3

Consider what the confusion costs at the margin. A safety warning scores as hostile. A risk disclosure scores as pessimism. A calm description of something dangerous scores, on arousal, as though nothing were at stake. Each error is small and each is systematic, in the same direction, for the same reason: the instrument reads the shadow and reports on the object. The Vermont team built a successor instrument for the new axes, an ousiometer, from the Greek ousia, essence, extending their earlier hedonometer that tracked the happiness of texts. Run over Les Misérables, it traces the novel as a path across the power and danger grid, section by section, the story told as a sequence of assessments.3

Systems made of language inherit the bias from the other direction as well. A model trained on the token stream learns the stream's proportions: the heavy rotation of the safe, the reserve kept for the dangerous. Rare and loud is the statistical signature of alarm in such a stream, which may be part of why alarms, fictional and real, carry the charge they do in text. The vocabulary of danger is stored energy. A literature that reached for it constantly would spend it.

Return to the orphan and her game. Pollyanna played at gladness deliberately, as a discipline, against the facts of a hard household. The hypothesis named after her mistook the species for a player of the same game, cheering itself by word choice. The recount suggests a different activity, older and less charming. The words lean safe because the speakers were checking: the strength of the thing in front of them, the harm it could do, whether the pattern would hold. Language was never smiling at the world. It was testing the floor before putting weight on it.
