The Count Has an Error Bar

On January 4, 2018, a Virginia election ended in a bowl: two film canisters, one name drawn. That was counting carried to its limit. Every vote total is a measurement, and the closest races are settled inside its noise by rules agreed before the noise arrived.

NOVEMBER 3, 2026

On January 4, 2018, in Richmond, the Virginia State Board of Elections put two names into two film canisters and the canisters into a bowl. The names were David Yancey and Shelly Simonds, candidates for a seat in the House of Delegates. The chairman drew one canister out. Inside was Yancey's name, and with it a 51 to 49 Republican majority in the chamber.1

The drawing looks like a surrender, the moment counting gave up. The sequence that led to the bowl says otherwise. Yancey had left election night ahead by ten votes. A recount put Simonds ahead by one, 11,608 to 11,607. A panel of judges then examined a ballot the recount had set aside: both names marked, with a slash through Simonds's mark. The judges read the slash as a correction and counted the ballot for Yancey. Eleven thousand six hundred and eight, each. Virginia law had a provision for exactly this, written long before either candidate was born: a tie is decided by lot.1

A ballot is a reading

That disputed ballot carries the whole problem in miniature. A vote is a mark made by a person, and a count is a series of decisions about what marks mean. Most marks are unambiguous. At the edges sit the dimpled chad, the bubble filled for two names and then crossed, the write-in spelled three ways. Somebody, or some machine set to a threshold, has to convert those marks into numbers, and the conversion rule is part of the result.

Florida made this impossible to ignore in 2000. The certified margin was 537 votes out of 5,963,110 cast. Afterwards the National Opinion Research Center examined the state's uncounted ballots under a range of standards for what counts as a vote. Under every standard it tested for a review of all uncounted ballots statewide, Gore finished ahead, by margins between 60 and 171 votes. Under the limited recounts requested or ordered at the time, Bush stayed ahead.2 Same ballots. Same marks. The winner changed with the rule for reading them, and the rule had to be chosen by people who already knew what was at stake.

A laboratory would print this honestly. The result would arrive as a range, with the method stated beside it and an uncertainty attached. An election prints an integer.

How wide the noise runs

The reading problem sits on top of a plainer one: counters miscount. Researchers at Rice and Clemson had teams count clearly marked ballots under the two procedures American precincts commonly use. The careful method, one official reading each ballot aloud while others watch and tally, still produced errors on half a percent to one percent of ballots. The faster method, sorting ballots into piles and counting the piles, ran as high as two percent.3 Those were laboratory conditions, with no ambiguous marks at all.

Set those rates beside the margins that decide real seats. In Minnesota in 2008, Al Franken took a Senate seat by 312 votes out of more than 2.9 million cast, a margin of about a hundredth of a percent. In Iowa in 2020, Mariannette Miller-Meeks was certified the winner of a House seat by six votes: 196,964 to 196,958.4 A one percent counting error, applied to either race, is a landslide by comparison. If counting were a single pass by tired people, races this close would carry no information at all.

Counting in practice survives because it is built to catch its own errors. Ballots are counted by machine, watched by partisans of both sides, retabulated in audits, and recounted under challenge. The errors that remain tend to point in both directions and cancel. In 2025, researchers at MIT and elsewhere assembled every available audit of the 2020 American elections: 856 jurisdictions, more than 71 million votes. Audits shifted the net presidential count by about 0.007 percent. The median discrepancy for a candidate was zero votes.5 In Georgia, the closest state that year, the margin was about 0.23 percent, more than thirty times the average shift an audit produced.

So the system is accurate in the way a good instrument is accurate. Repeated readings agree closely, and the disagreement that remains is small, random, and measurable. What the agreement cannot do is shrink to zero. There is a floor below which another recount stops adding information and starts sampling the noise: a different team, a different threshold for a faint mark, a pile counted in a different order. Past that floor, asking who won has the same structure as asking for the eleventh decimal of a measurement taken with a ruler.

The audit that states its limit

One corner of election administration has started printing the error bar. A risk-limiting audit examines a random sample of paper ballots and asks a narrower question than who won: how strong is the evidence that the reported winner is the real one? The audit is built around a number chosen in advance, the risk limit. At a five percent limit, if the reported outcome is wrong, the audit has at least a 95 percent chance of catching that fact and escalating to a full hand count. Colorado began with a nine percent limit and has worked down to three.6

Notice what the guarantee covers. It bounds the chance of certifying a wrong outcome. It offers no claim that the certified total is the true total, down to the last ballot. The sample grows as the margin shrinks, and in a close enough race the sample becomes the whole pile, counted by hand, with the hand-count error rates from the laboratory waiting at the bottom of the procedure. The statistics run out at exactly the place the bowl was waiting in Virginia.

This is the mature form of the thing. A young institution promises an exact count. An older one specifies, in public and in advance, how it will decide, how it will check the decision, and what it will do when the decision sits inside the margin where checking stops helping. The rules for the bad case are written during the ordinary case, by people who do not yet know whose name the bad case will carry.

Tonight, the integers arrive

Today is election day in the United States, and by tonight the totals will scroll past as integers, one per candidate, no ranges attached. For almost every race on the ballot that presentation is earned. The margins will sit far outside any plausible counting noise, and the integer, while technically a reading, is a reading nobody reasonable contests.

Somewhere down the ballot, though, a school board seat or a statehouse race will land at forty votes, or six, or dead even. Then the machinery behind the integer becomes visible: the recount with its stated standard for a stray mark, the audit with its stated risk, the deadline after which the result stands regardless. And at the end of the line, in more states than Virginia, a provision for deciding by lot. A coin. A drawing. A name in a canister.

The canister tends to be reported as an embarrassment, a failure of the count to do its job. Run the sequence backwards and it reads differently. The count was taken, retaken under challenge, examined ballot by ballot until two honest readings of the same marks produced the same number for both names. At that point the election had extracted everything the ballots contained. What remained was a decision, and the law supplied one that neither side could operate, tilt, or recount its way around: chance, administered in public, under a rule both sides had accepted before anyone knew it would be needed.

A count ends when the agreed procedure says it ends, and the ending is the result. The bowl belongs in the same drawer as the tally sheet. Both are instruments for finishing.
