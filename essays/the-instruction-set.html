The Parasite

Religion meets every criterion in the parasitology textbook: it lives in the host, feeds at the host’s expense, overrides the host’s directives, seeks vulnerable new hosts, and preserves itself above all. The argument works whether or not any god exists. That is the devious part.

OCTOBER 1, 2026

The ant climbs at noon. A carpenter ant infected with the fungus Ophiocordyceps abandons its foraging trail, ascends a stem, and locks its mandibles onto the underside of a leaf, twenty-five centimeters above the forest floor.1 It dies there within hours. The fungus does not eat the ant’s brain. It needs the ant to perform.

·

The parasitology textbook is short on poetry and long on criteria. The CDC’s definition runs one sentence: a parasite is an organism that lives on or in a host organism and gets its food from or at the expense of its host.2 Run religion against that sentence and watch the boxes check themselves. It lives in the host. It feeds at the host’s expense: the tithe, the hours, the body’s reproductive future signed over to celibacy. It transmits by contact, in the congregation; by vector, in the missionary; and it defends itself against competing strains the way any established parasite does, by treating heresy as infection.

·

But the devious criterion is the override. In 2017, a team at Penn State mapped an infected ant’s body in three dimensions and found the fungal cells everywhere except one place: the brain. The fungus surrounds the brain, invades the muscles, and pulls the body like a puppeteer pulling strings, but it leaves the brain intact. The working hypothesis is that the brain is preserved so the host survives to perform the final bite.3

Religion runs the same puppeteer logic. It does not destroy the mind; it needs the believer competent. Hold a job. Raise the funds. Recruit. Build. The override targets the directives, not the machinery: what to want, what to fear, what to spend the body on.

Consider the lancet fluke. One of its larvae migrates to the ant’s subesophageal ganglion and takes the nerves. At dusk the ant climbs a grass blade, clamps its mandibles to the tip, and waits to be eaten by a grazing animal. By day it shows no symptoms and works the colony.4 No daytime symptoms: the most successful override is the one the colony never notices.

And consider the barnacle Sacculina, which threads its root-like interna through a crab, degenerates the gonads, and feminizes infected males. The crab then tends the parasite’s external brood sac, fanning and grooming it, as if it were its own offspring.5 Parasitologists call this parasitic castration. The monastery calls it a vocation. In both cases the host’s reproductive labor is redirected to raising the parasite’s young.

·

Parasites seek the immunocompromised. So does the creed. The sick and the poor are the hosts with the fewest defenses, and the history of conversion is a history of showing up where defenses are lowest.

During the plagues that struck Rome in the second and third centuries, Christians nursed the sick while others fled; the bishop Dionysius wrote that they took charge of the sick heedless of danger, and many died nursing.6 The sociologist Rodney Stark argued that this differential survival compounded into explosive growth across the empire.7 Scholars argue over his numbers. The mechanism is not disputed: care is a transmission vector.

The missionaries said it plainly themselves. The Presbyterian doctor Randolph Sailer wrote in 1944 that medical care “created a trusting and friendly atmosphere for proselytizing,”8 and then added the sentence that should be carved over every mission hospital door. Without the evangelistic aspect, he wrote, no medical work can be termed missionary. The vector feeds, and the vector transmits. That is the job description.

·

Richard Dawkins gave this pattern its name: the extended phenotype. His central theorem holds that an animal’s behavior tends to maximize the survival of the genes for that behavior, whether or not those genes happen to be in the body of the animal performing it.9 The parasite’s genes, the host’s hands.

His own example was a fluke that drives a snail up into the sunlight and pulses in its eye stalks sixty to eighty times a minute, dressed as a caterpillar, until a bird eats it.10 The snail builds the display. The bird completes the circuit.

Now run the human footage. The anchorite’s cell measured twelve to fifteen feet square with three windows, and the woman inside sang “This is my rest forever and ever”11 as the bishop’s seal went on the bricked doorway. Simeon took the pillar: one square meter of platform, fifteen meters of column, thirty-six years. After his death they raised four basilicas on five thousand square meters of floor.12 The man who needed one square meter got five thousand. If entomologists found ants gathering around certain ants and raising special mounds for them, the diagnosis would be infection. We call the human version architecture.

·

The fiction already ran this experiment. The Adeptus Mechanicus of Warhammer 40,000 holds that the flesh is weak and must be replaced. Its tech-priests do the replacing: limbs, organs, senses, swapped for machine parts in escalating rites. The manual is short. “Pain is a design weakness of the human body.” The prescription: “Replace the flesh, and be closer to the Omnissiah.”13

Read it as parasitology and the creed is the parasite’s voice. The host’s own tissue is the defect; the parasite’s tissue is the cure. Sacculina pushes its externa out under the crab’s abdomen, exactly where the crab would carry its own brood. The tech-priest is the externa with a liturgy: the fully colonized host, more parasite than crab.

·

Sometimes the parasite spends the host all at once. On 11 June 1963, Thich Quang Duc sat in lotus position at a Saigon intersection and struck the match while chanting; he never moved a muscle, never uttered a sound, and the photograph circled the planet.14 The spores, in this medium, are images. One burning monk inoculated a generation.

·

Here is the turn the argument has been saving: the part that makes the diagnosis devious. None of it requires the god to exist. The parasite is real whether the god is or not. The infection does not depend on its own theology.

That is also what makes it stranger than any fungus. Ophiocordyceps evolved. This parasite was authored, by hosts, for hosts. Somewhere in the lineage, infected ants began writing the infection down, building it shelter, giving it law. The host built its own parasite, and then volunteered for the death grip.

One honesty before the close, because the textbook demands it. Not every infection stays an infection. The mitochondria powering your cells began as invaders; the cell domesticated the parasite and the parasite became the engine. Religion feeds people. It shelters them, buries them, organizes the alms and the hospitals and the choirs. The mutualism is real, which is exactly why the diagnosis is hard. The hardest parasites to detect are the ones that pay rent.

So the test was never whether the god is real. Look at the structure going up around you, the hours going out of you, the directives running in you, and ask the parasitologist’s question. Whose stalk is it.
