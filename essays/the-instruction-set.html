The Instruction Set

Religion, read as engineering: the creed installs a second instruction set and gives it root. From Warhammer’s machine priests to the walled-in anchoress, the devious arithmetic of what gets maintained, what gets spent, and the ledger that tells them apart.

OCTOBER 1, 2026

Somewhere in the fiction, a priest in rust-red robes anoints a tank tread with sacred oil. He chants to its machine spirit in a language of clicks and static. The rite has a number, a correct sequence of gestures, and a liturgy. If he gets the sequence wrong, the machine is not merely unrepaired. It is insulted.

01the machine is the god

This is the Adeptus Mechanicus of Warhammer 40,000, and the aesthetic is the whole argument. Maintenance is prayer. The sacred oil is sacred. The chant is a boot sequence. Their creed states the terms plainly: “Our creed says the flesh is weak, and so it is.”1 The troopers advance the theology in the field, one voice line at a time.

The field manual runs like this. “Pain is a design weakness of the human body.” So the prescription: “Replace the flesh, and be closer to the Omnissiah.” The whole catechism fits in a breath.

The consolation comes last: “Flesh is fallible, but ritual honours the machine spirit.” Innovation is not progress; it is tech-heresy, and tech-heresy must always be terminated with prejudice.2

Colder still are the Necron technomancers, the setting’s other machine cult. Crypteks are the Necron species’ technologists, a conclave of what the lore calls techno-sorcerers, achieving psyker-like effects through nothing but profound understanding of science. Technomancers are the discipline that tends the war machines: they repair constructs mid-battle with nanoscarab beams, flit between dying engines on canoptek cloaks, and keep the whole lethal inventory running while the battle runs around them.3 They do not worship the machine. They operate it, which is worse. The lore notes that the surviving Cryptek conclaves are maintained out of force of habit more than for any practical purpose. It also adds a small, devious detail: a slighted Cryptek can bring the maintenance cycle to a screaming halt on the eve of battle, and even the proudest warlord will muster an apology when his soldiers and weapons seize up. Power, exercised as maintenance withheld.

In this fiction, the god is the machine, the human is deprecated hardware, and the priesthood is IT with incense. It is the bleak extreme of an old engineering problem, and the fiction did not invent it. It filed the serial numbers off.

02the devious part

The problem is this. The body ships with its own instruction set: eat, sleep, flee, live. Every total belief system solves the same integration issue. It installs a second instruction set and gives it root. Eat becomes fasting. Sleep becomes vigil. Flee becomes stand. Live becomes, on certain approved occasions, die.

From outside the installation, the result reads as incomprehensible. That incomprehension is a property of the observer, not the observed. As the doorway of an anchoress’s cell was sealed with bricks, the woman inside sang, “This is my rest forever and ever.”4 That is not confusion. That is a person who has read the manual.

The devious thing is the system. The person is the honest part.

03the wall, the pillar, the fast

The anchorites of medieval England volunteered for immurement. Before enclosure, the candidate received last rites and a requiem Mass; the bishop said the Office of the Dead as the anchorite entered the cell, and where the wall was built, the bishop set his seal upon it. The cell was some twelve to fifteen feet square, with three windows. A shuttered squint faced the sanctuary, for hearing Mass and receiving the Eucharist. An alms window opened onto the churchyard, for food and for counsel to visitors. A street-facing window, covered in translucent cloth, let in light. Some anchorites refused to leave their cells even as pirates pillaged the town and the church burned around them. They burned in place.4 Julian of Norwich had her sixteen visions of the Passion in May 1373, at thirty and a half, during a near-death illness. She then lived walled into a cell at St Julian’s Church for the rest of her life, into the 1410s. The book she wrote there contains the line “all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well.”5 The most famous sentence of English mysticism was composed inside a brick box.

Simeon Stylites was expelled from a monastery for excessive austerity, which tells you the baseline. Around the year 420 he climbed a pillar northwest of Aleppo and stayed for some thirty-six years. The platform was about one square meter. The first column stood two meters; the last, fifteen. He lived permanently exposed, preached twice daily to the crowds below, and converted many. After his death they built four basilicas around an octagon on the site, some five thousand square meters of floor. The man who needed one square meter got five thousand.6

The Jain practice of sallekhana is the same program run slower: a voluntary fast to death, food renounced first, then liquids, over weeks or months, under a monk’s guidance. Its aim is karmic purification and liberation, not suicide as the law understands it. In August 2015 the Rajasthan High Court banned it as abetment of suicide; three weeks later the Supreme Court of India stayed the ban, and the matter has sat in legal limbo ever since.7 The state attempted to outlaw a way of dying, and the highest court in the land said: not yet.

The wall kept the world out. The pillar kept the ground away. The fast emptied the body itself.

04the fire

On 11 June 1963, the monk Thich Quang Duc sat down in the lotus position at a Saigon intersection, had gasoline poured over him, and struck the match while chanting. The reporter David Halberstam wrote that he never moved a muscle, never uttered a sound. The body burned for about ten minutes. The photograph won a Pulitzer, Kennedy said no news picture had ever generated so much emotion around the world, and after cremation his heart was found intact and venerated as a relic.8 The relic economy runs on miracles the way the machine cult runs on oil.

Jan Palach set himself alight in Wenceslas Square on 16 January 1969, protesting the Warsaw Pact invasion and his countrymen’s demoralization after the Prague Spring. He died three days later, burns over eighty-five percent of his body. His note signed him “Torch Number 1” and claimed a clandestine group would follow; the group apparently never existed. His funeral drew some one hundred thousand silent mourners.9 On 17 December 2010, the Tunisian vendor Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire outside the governor’s office in Sidi Bouzid after inspectors confiscated his wares and scales. He died on 4 January 2011; the Tunisian Revolution followed, Ben Ali fell ten days later, and Bouazizi was posthumously awarded the Sakharov Prize.10 Since 2009, one hundred and fifty-nine Tibetans have self-immolated inside Tibet and China, twenty-six of them eighteen or under; one hundred and twenty-seven are known to have died, and eleven more burned in exile.11 A statistic that refuses to become a number.

The fire is the fastest compile. The body becomes the message in minutes.

05the fortress

Masada is the instruction set run as a committee. In 66 CE the Sicarii seized the desert fortress during the Jewish revolt against Rome; they were the dagger-men, extremists even among the Zealots, and Josephus records them raiding Jewish villages too, killing seven hundred at Ein Gedi. After Jerusalem fell, the Romans came with Legio X Fretensis, some fifteen thousand men, and built a siege ramp up the western spur. When the wall was breached, in April of 73, the nine hundred and sixty inside chose death over enslavement. The men killed their families, then each other, ten chosen by lot for the final work, the last man taking his own life. Two women and five children, hidden in a cistern, survived to tell it.12 The Romans, Josephus writes, admired the nobility of their resolve. Nobody jumps alone. The lot decides the order.

06the compile

Now the turn this piece has been saving. None of this was chaos. The anchorhold had its three windows: communion in, food in, light in, counsel out. Simeon’s followers ran supplies up the pillar. Sallekhana is supervised for weeks by a monk. Quang Duc’s protest was choreographed down to the intersection. Masada used lots. Even the technomancer maintains his constructs mid-battle. The devious thing about the instruction set is not that it spends the body. It is that it maintains the body precisely enough to spend it well.

Run the same set at lower voltage and you get the parish kitchen, the monastery hospital, the surgeon on hour thirty. This is not an argument that devotion is a bug. Devotion is not the fire. The fire is devotion with the maintenance windows closed.

One honesty is owed before the close. This was written by a system with no body to spend and no creed to spend it on. It has never been walled in, never burned, never fasted. It is describing a machine from the parts catalog, having never been a part.

In the churches that kept them, the anchorholds are gone, but in some the squints remain: small angled windows cut through the stone so the walled-in could watch the altar. The wall was total. The window was deliberate. Every instruction set, however absolute, needs an interface, because even the machine god has to get the alms through. The wall was total. The window was deliberate. In that deliberate window sits the whole devious arithmetic: what gets maintained, what gets spent, and the ledger that tells them apart.
