The Lie Became the Landmark

Two mapmakers invented a hamlet in the Catskills to catch copycats and printed it on Esso road maps. A fishing lodge took the name, a rival pleaded county records, and in 2014 the federal government filed the fiction in its official gazetteer, marked not official.

NOVEMBER 5, 2026

North of Roscoe, New York, where Route 206 crosses Morton Hill Road, the Catskills do what they do everywhere: trees, a dirt shoulder, weather. For most of the twentieth century, the Esso road maps of New York showed a hamlet at that crossing. Its name was Agloe. There was no hamlet. There was barely a crossing. The name was the whole settlement, and the name was a signature.1

A map is a strange property. The roads on it belong to everyone; any driver can check them for the price of gasoline. Yet the finished map, the selection and arrangement of ten thousand public facts, is a work, and works get stolen. A rival who lifts your survey inherits your errors too, but an error is hard to prove in court, because the world is under no obligation to match your map. Unless you plant one.

A signature you could sue over

In the 1920s, Otto G. Lindberg, founder of the General Drafting Company, and his assistant Ernest Alpers planted one at that crossing. They scrambled their initials, O.G.L. and E.A., into a name with no history and no other plausible author.1 General Drafting drew the road maps Esso gave away at its stations, and Agloe rode in glove compartments across the Northeast for decades, a town that existed exactly where a map said it did and nowhere else.

The logic was airtight. A real town might turn up on any honest map. Agloe could turn up on only one kind, a copied one. It was evidence engineered in advance, a fact whose sole function was to be false in a traceable way.

The defense was a fishing lodge

In 1930 a company called Agloe Lodge Farms filed its incorporation papers in New York.2 It bought a fishing lodge near the crossing and renamed it Agloe Lodge. The family that sold the land, for one dollar, told a reporter decades later that they had suspected the buyer was a front for Rand McNally, General Drafting's great rival.3 Nobody has ever proven that. Nobody has had to disprove it either.

Years afterward, Agloe turned up on a Rand McNally map. General Drafting objected, on solid ground: their invention, their trap, sprung. Rand McNally answered that its cartographers had copied nothing. They had consulted Delaware County records, and the county records listed a business named Agloe at that spot.4

The trap had been built to separate two kinds of mapmaker, those who survey and those who copy. It failed because the world had joined the second group. Somewhere between the Esso station and the county clerk, the fiction had been taken at face value and written into the paperwork of the place itself. After that, copying the map and surveying the ground produced the same entry, and nobody could tell the two apart.

The best-known version of this story, the one with a general store whose owners read the name off an Esso map and hung it over their door, is also the shakiest. Cartographer Frank Brown told it that way to a collectors' association in 2002. People who lived near the crossing said there was never a general store there. There was a fishing lodge.4 The lodge is enough. A business took the invented name, the county wrote it down, and the record stopped asking where the name had come from.

Filed as a place

Agloe held its place on maps into the 1990s, long after the lodge was gone, because maps copy maps and deletion is the one edit nobody schedules. It surfaced briefly on Google Maps. Then, on February 25, 2014, the United States Geological Survey entered Agloe in the Geographic Names Information System, the federal gazetteer of American places, with a parenthesis doing the honest work: Agloe (Not Official).5

Consider what that entry is. The government's list of real names now carries an invented one, flagged as invented, because the invention had gathered too much history to leave out. Readers of John Green's 2008 novel Paper Towns, which sends its runaway to Agloe, had been driving to the crossing for years and leaving copies of the book behind them.6 The gazetteer was not describing a settlement. It was describing a pilgrimage. The parenthesis is the sound of a record admitting what it is for.

The same loop, faster

In September 2008, Mike Nolan ran web services at Edge Hill University in Lancashire. He noticed a village on Google Maps that he had somehow never driven through: Argleton, sitting in the fields outside Aughton.7 There were no streets and no houses. There was only the label. Within months the label had a working life. Businesses in the postcode were listed as Argleton businesses. Weather was reported for Argleton. Jobs were advertised in Argleton. Roy Bayfield walked out to the coordinates in February 2009 and reported the field deceptively normal.8 By May 2010 Google had taken the village off the map. The data provider, Tele Atlas, could not explain how it got in.7

Agloe needed half a century, a lodge, and a county clerk to become almost real. Argleton needed one database entry and a season. Nothing was built in Lancashire; no one hung a sign. The records recruited each other directly, listing citing listing, until a field had a job market and a forecast. Lindberg and Alpers planted their fiction by hand and waited for one business to adopt it. The newer version requires no adoption at all. It requires only that the next system in line trust the previous one, which is the one thing every system in line is built to do.

There is a crossing north of Roscoe where the federal government keeps a name it does not believe in, because leaving it out would now be the larger falsehood. Every large record of the world contains some number of Agloes: entries whose evidence is other entries, labels the ground never confirmed and the paperwork no longer questions. The honest ones carry the parenthesis. Most do not. And somewhere in the stack beneath every map you will ever use, a clerk, a lodge, or a database has already decided which names will be waiting when you arrive.

1.Agloe, New York: the hamlet was placed by General Drafting founder Otto G. Lindberg and his assistant Ernest Alpers at the intersection of NY 206 and Morton Hill Road, north of Roscoe, as a copyright trap on the company's Esso road maps; the name is an anagram of their initials. See "Agloe, New York," en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agloe,_New_York, and John Lackie, "Copyright traps," New Scientist, November 25, 2006.

2."Agloe Lodge Farms Files Incorporation Certificate," Binghamton Press, May 1, 1930, p. 30. The company acquired a fishing lodge in the area and renamed it Agloe Lodge.

3."The strange story of Agloe, NY," Times Herald-Record (2016): members of the Nead family, which sold the land to Agloe Lodge Farms for $1, said they suspected the company was a front for Rand McNally. The suspicion is reported, not established.

4.The Rand McNally episode and the general-store version come from cartographer Frank Brown, as recounted to the Road Map Collectors' Association in 2002 and reported by Ian Byrne, "Errors on road maps," Petrol Maps (March 19, 2006). Longtime residents told the Times Herald-Record there was never a general store at the site; the business was the fishing lodge renamed Agloe Lodge.

5."Agloe (Not Official)," Geographic Names Information System, United States Geological Survey, entered February 25, 2014. Agloe continued to appear on road maps into the 1990s before deletion, and surfaced briefly on Google Maps.

6.John Green, Paper Towns (2008); the visits of readers to the intersection are recounted in Robert Krulwich, "An Imaginary Town Becomes Real, Then Not. True Story," NPR, March 18, 2014, and Sam Roberts, "Seeking a Town on the Border of Fiction and Reality," The New York Times, March 29, 2014. Agloe is also the MacGuffin of Peng Shepherd's novel The Cartographers (2022).

7.Argleton, West Lancashire: a phantom settlement that appeared on Google Maps and Google Earth in the fields of Aughton parish, first written about by Mike Nolan of Edge Hill University in September 2008. Services reusing Google data treated it as a real settlement in the L39 postcode area, ascribing local businesses and weather reports to it. Google's data provider Tele Atlas was unable to explain the entry; the location was removed by May 2010. See "Argleton," en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argleton.

8.Roy Bayfield, a colleague of Nolan's, walked to the Argleton coordinates in February 2009. "Deceptively normal" is his description of the field he found there, quoted in coverage of the phantom village since, including The Guardian's.
